Key Developments

Project Flytrap 5.0 at Pabradė: unit-led drone-warfare TTPs at squadron scale

In May 2026, the 2nd Cavalry Regiment ran Project Flytrap 5.0 at Pabradė Training Area, Lithuania, integrating over 20 pieces of counter-UAS and friendly-UAS equipment at troop and squadron level with Strykers, unmanned ground vehicles, and AI-enabled operating systems. The exercise developed new TTPs for synchronising intelligence, fires, and maneuver under a multi-layered drone threat. Flytrap 6.0 is expected to scale further.

Drone-warfare integration has moved from contractor-led demonstrations to unit-led training. The TTPs being generated by 2nd Cavalry will perish without an active capture mechanism into TRADOC doctrine and CTC opposing-force playbooks. JMRC and JRTC should mirror the Flytrap architecture before they fall behind.

Ivy Mass red-team event tests NGC2 at division scale

The 4th Infantry Division's May 2026 Ivy Mass exercise puts the next-generation command-and-control (NGC2) prototype against an Army Training and Transformation Command red team for the first time at division level. The Continuous Transformation acquisition model has more than a dozen vendors including Anduril and Palantir building the system while soldiers test it in the field.

If NGC2 survives Ivy Mass, the training enterprise needs to reorient on a 90-day cadence, not annual cycles. Every CTC rotation, training management system, and simulator becomes downstream of decisions made at Fort Carson this month. The Continuous Transformation model only works if TDDG can close the feedback loop from soldier use into next-iteration training scenarios fast enough.

SOCOM leaders disclose heavy generative-AI use; conventional force lags

At SOF Week 2026 in Tampa (May 19-22), SOCOM program executives stated that special operators already use generative AI 'heavily' for resource allocation and force deployment, and are pursuing AI agents and voice command for tactical operations. The current target is smaller models running at the tactical edge under fog-computing architectures, sourced from smaller startups rather than incumbent vendors.

SOF is operating ahead of the conventional force on AI for staff work, not for tactical lethality. The gap is institutional: conventional battalion and brigade staffs have neither training nor governance. If TDDG waits for the binding constraint to be obvious, it will be 18 to 24 months behind the use case.

Army Aviation's lost decade reframed as a PME problem

Jake Steckler, writing in War on the Rocks on 18 May 2026, argues Army Aviation's decade-long effort to integrate Apaches with Shadow drones (ended in 2025) failed not because of procurement but because of cultural inertia, talent-model mismatch between manned and unmanned crews, and an evaluation system that rewarded reporting success over identifying failure. He proposes the Captain's Career Course add critical assessment of emerging technologies as a graded competency.

This is the most actionable PME prescription published this month. The Captain's Career Course is a TRADOC institution; an FY27 pilot block on emerging-tech assessment is feasible inside one budget cycle. The proposal also extends to the Unmanned Advanced Lethality Course (three weeks today vs Ukraine's 30+ schools running multi-month programmes).

Cross-Cutting Themes

TDDG is one institutional cycle behind unit-led and SOF-led adoption of emerging tech

Three of this month's four articles describe the same pattern from different angles: 2nd Cavalry Regiment producing drone-warfare TTPs at squadron level in Lithuania; SOF using generative AI heavily for staff work while conventional formations have neither training nor doctrine for it; and Army Aviation's decade-long inability to absorb a manned-unmanned teaming concept that worked in 2006. The common failure mode is institutional latency, not technological readiness. The remedy is not more procurement but faster capture of unit-level lessons into PME, CTC playbooks, and training infrastructure.
